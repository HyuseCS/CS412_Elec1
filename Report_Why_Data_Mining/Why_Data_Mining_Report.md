# Why Data Mining?

## Introduction

We live in what the book by Han, Kamber and Pei calls the "information age", which many people also call the "data age". Almost every part of life now leaves a record. This report explains why data mining is needed. It follows Chapter 1 of *Data Mining: Concepts and Techniques* (Han, Kamber and Pei, 2012). The report has three parts: the problem of too much data, the answer that data mining gives, and three examples of how it is used.

## The Explosive Growth of Data

The book describes "the explosive growth of data". This means that the amount of data in the world is growing very fast, not slowly. Many things cause this. Businesses record every sale and every customer. Scientists and engineers record the results of experiments and measurements. Hospitals keep records of patients. Web searches, social media and mobile phones add more data every day. Powerful computers and cheap storage make it easy to collect and keep all of it.

So the first reason for data mining is the size of the data. There is far more data than any person or team can read.

## Data Rich but Information Poor

Having a lot of data does not mean we know a lot. The book says that we are "data rich but information poor". We store huge amounts of data, but we often cannot tell what it means. Older tools can store data and answer simple questions about it. They do not find new patterns by themselves.

Because of this, large data archives can turn into what the book calls "data tombs". Data goes into them, but nobody looks at it again. This is a waste. The data may hold facts that could help a business, a hospital or a scientist. The facts are there, but they are hidden. The need is clear: we need tools that can look through large data and bring out what is useful.

## Data Mining: From Data to Knowledge

Data mining is the answer to this need. It is the process of finding interesting patterns and useful knowledge in large amounts of data. The book notes that a better name might be "knowledge mining from data". It also links data mining to knowledge discovery from data, or KDD. KDD is the whole process. It includes preparing the data, finding the patterns, and checking and showing the results. Data mining is the key step of that process.

The book uses the picture of mining gold from rock or sand. The raw data is like the rock. It is not what we want. The knowledge inside the data is the gold. Data mining is the work of digging it out. A computer can search very large data fast, and it can find patterns that a person would never see by hand.

## Examples

**Stores: market basket analysis.** A store can study what customers buy together. If some items often appear in the same basket, the store can put them close together or offer a deal on both. This helps the store sell more, and it helps the customer.

**Banks: credit card fraud detection.** A bank can study a large number of card payments and learn what normal spending looks like. When a payment looks very different from the normal pattern, the system can flag it as possible fraud. No person could check every payment, but a data mining system can.

**Hospitals: patient risk prediction.** A hospital can study past patient records to find which patients may be at higher risk. Doctors and nurses can then give extra care early to the patients who need it most.

In all three cases, the data already existed. Data mining turned it into knowledge that people could act on.

## Conclusion

Data is growing very fast, and people cannot read all of it. This leaves us data rich but information poor, with a risk of data tombs. Data mining solves this problem. It turns raw data into useful knowledge, as gold is taken from rock or sand. Stores, banks and hospitals already use it to make better decisions. This is why data mining is needed.
