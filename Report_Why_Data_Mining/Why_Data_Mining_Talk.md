# Why Data Mining - 5 Minute Talk

Source: Han, Kamber and Pei, *Data Mining: Concepts and Techniques*, Chapter 1.

How to use this script: read the plain text aloud. Lines in [brackets] are cues for what to do or
where to point. Do not read them aloud. Speak at a calm pace, about 140 words per minute. The time
marks show where you should be.

## Slide 1: Data Rich, Information Poor (0:00-1:30)

- Explosive growth: stores, labs, hospitals and phones record data all day
- Too much to read: old tools store data but cannot say what it means
- Data tombs: data goes in, and nobody looks at it again
- "We live in the information age, also called the data age."

**Script:**
[Lines in brackets are cues. Do not read them aloud.]

[Slide 1 is on the screen. Look at the class and wait two seconds before you start.]

Good day, everyone. My topic is "Why Data Mining". It comes from Chapter 1 of the book Data Mining: Concepts and Techniques, by Han, Kamber and Pei. In the next five minutes, I want to answer one question: why do we need data mining at all?

[Point to the title.]

The title of this slide is a phrase from the book: "data rich, but information poor". These three boxes explain what it means.

[Point to the first box, "Explosive growth".]

First, explosive growth. The book says we live in the information age, but a better name is the data age. Stores record every sale. Labs record every experiment. Hospitals record every patient. Our phones make new data all day.

[Point to the second box, "Too much to read".]

Second, there is too much to read. No person can go through millions of records. Our old tools can store and search data, but they cannot tell us what it means.

[Point to the third box, "Data tombs".]

Third, data tombs. Data goes in, and nobody looks at it again. Useful patterns stay buried inside.

[Pause. Point to the gold line at the bottom.]

So we have more data than ever, but not more understanding. That is the problem.

[Click to slide 2.]

## Slide 2: Turning Data into Knowledge (1:30-3:00)

- Collect and clean the data, find the patterns, check and show them, get useful knowledge
- KDD: knowledge discovery from data; data mining is its key step
- A better name: "knowledge mining from data"
- Like mining gold from rock or sand: we want the gold, not the rock

**Script:**
[Wait for slide 2 to show.]

So what is the answer? Data mining. Data mining means finding interesting patterns and useful knowledge in large amounts of data.

[Move your hand along the four boxes, from left to right.]

These four boxes show the whole process. The book calls it knowledge discovery from data, or KDD. First, we collect and clean the data, because real data has errors and gaps. Second, we find the patterns. This is the data mining step. Third, we check the patterns and show them in a way people understand. And at the end, in the dark box, we get useful knowledge.

[Point to the box at the bottom left.]

The book says a better name for data mining would be "knowledge mining from data". We do not want the data itself. We want the knowledge inside it.

[Point to the gold box at the bottom right.]

That is why the book compares it to mining gold from rock or sand. A miner does not want the rock. A miner wants the gold. A computer can dig through far more data than a person, and it can find patterns a person would never see.

[Click to slide 3.]

## Slide 3: Data Mining at Work (3:00-4:45)

- Stores: market basket analysis
- Banks: fraud detection
- Hospitals: patient risk prediction
- "The data was already there. Data mining made it useful."

**Script:**
[Wait for slide 3 to show.]

Now, three places where data mining is used today.

[Point to the first box, "Stores".]

First, stores. A store can look at what customers buy together. This is called market basket analysis. If some items often sell together, the store can put them close on the shelf, or offer a deal on both.

[Point to the second box, "Banks".]

Second, banks. A bank learns how each customer normally spends. When a card payment looks very different, the system flags it as possible fraud, so the bank can check it fast. No person could watch every payment, but a data mining system can.

[Point to the third box, "Hospitals".]

Third, hospitals. A hospital can study past patient records and find which patients are at higher risk. Then doctors and nurses can act early and give extra care to the patients who need it most.

[Point to the gold line at the bottom.]

In all three cases, the data was already there. The store had its sales, the bank had its payments, and the hospital had its records. Data mining made that data useful.

[Pause. Look at the class.]

To sum up: data is growing very fast. No person can read it all, so we are data rich but information poor. Data mining fixes this. It turns raw data into useful knowledge. That is why we need it.

Thank you for listening. I am happy to take any questions.

[Stop at about 4:45. This leaves time before 5:00.]
